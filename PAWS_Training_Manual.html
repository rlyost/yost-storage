Foundations

The Three-Layer Method

Every new behavior gets built in the same three layers. Never skip a layer.

  1. Lure. Use a treat in your hand to guide the dog into the behavior. The moment the behavior is complete, say Yes and give the treat. Repeat until reliable.
  2. Hand motion only. Same hand motion, no treat in your hand. When the dog performs it, say Yes, then reach into the pouch and reward. Repeat until reliable.
  3. Verbal command. Give only the word. When the dog performs it, say Yes and reward. Practice until the response is automatic.
How to advanceMove up a layer only when the current layer is reliable. When the dog struggles, step back down a layer. Stepping backward is a tool, not a failure.

Training Wheels On, Then Off

How the handler's role changes as the dog advances.

Early sessions are training wheels on. The handler carries the instruction: exact timing of the treat, the verbal command, the hand signal, every question answered for the dog.

Later sessions take the wheels off. The dog does roughly 80% of the effort and the handler supplies encouragement only. You are telling the dog lean left, lean right. The dog has to balance itself.

Why it mattersLearning has to become self-exploratory. A dog that only performs when its paw is held is not showing you it can do the task. Put it in situations where it proves capability on its own, then support without answering.

What changes in practice

  • Commands go from repeated and guided to said once.
  • That's it replaces re-cueing as your main tool.
  • You stop steering the dog's path and start fueling the choice you want.
  • Difficulty rises through duration, distance, and distraction rather than through new instruction.

The "Yes" Marker

One word that stamps a moment in time and tells the dog exactly what earned the reward.

How to use it

  1. Watch for the exact behavior you want.
  2. Say Yes at the instant it happens, not a second later.
  3. Deliver the treat immediately after.
Never break this"Yes" is a promise. Every "Yes" gets followed by a reward, every time. Break it once and the marker loses meaning.
Timing trapsIf you mark while the dog is staring at your treat pouch, you taught the dog that staring at the pouch pays. Mark eye contact with you, mark solid contact on the touch target, mark the completed behavior. Not the moment before it.
Marking too early on a retrieve"Yes" means food is coming. A dog only has room for one thing in its mouth, so it will spit the item out to make room. Mark the handover, not the pickup. If you mark early, re-engage with That's it and keep the dog on task rather than restarting.

The Two-Second Pause

Impulse control built into the reward itself.

For a driven, food-motivated dog, the moment you say Yes is when tension spikes. It will nudge the pouch, whine, huff, or shove forward to speed you up. Slowing the delivery teaches the dog that the treat is guaranteed and rushing you changes nothing.

The four-second interval

  1. Say the dog's name. Wait for the response.
  2. Say Yes.
  3. Freeze. Do not move or reach for two full seconds.
  4. Reach into the pouch and take the treat. The dog will activate the moment it sees your hand move.
  5. Pause again before bringing the treat forward.
  6. Deliver. If you move too fast, the dog's nose is in the pouch before you finish.
What this teachesShort, repeatable patience. The dog learns to process the delay instead of trying to control the moment. It is a small detail with large downstream value.
Snowball riskLeft unaddressed, nudging the treat pouch generalizes. The same impatience shows up as obsession with other dogs, tennis balls, smells, and sounds. Correct it at the smallest scale.

Corrections Without Punishment

Two tools. Neither carries a negative consequence.

Tool 1 · Stillness

  1. The dog is searching for a reaction. Any reaction is the payoff.
  2. Become a statue. No eye contact, no voice, no movement.
  3. The dog keeps trying, gets nothing, and concludes the effort is not worth its time.
  4. Over repeated sessions the dog stops offering the behavior with you at all.
Environment limitsIgnoring works in a training room where noise can be expressed safely. In a townhouse with shared walls, a reactive dog barking, lunging, and destroying is not something the environment can absorb. Adapt the technique to where the dog actually lives.

Tool 2 · The verbal marker

  1. Use Nope, Don't, or Leave it in an assertive, serious voice.
  2. Say it once, with full confidence in what you want.
  3. The moment the dog makes the correct choice, mark it with Yes and reward.
Why it is not punishmentYou asked for something and the dog gave it to you, so you rewarded the choice. There is no negative cloud hanging over it. It is an instruction, not a consequence.

Handling in a Group

Multiple inputs read as pressure.

When two or more people give hand signals or verbal commands at the same time, the dog gets confused and excitement-driven mistakes go up. For a high-energy dog this compounds an already elevated state. For a safety-seeking dog it reads as pressure coming from all directions and lowers confidence.

Group protocol

  • Avoid stacking inputs. Two people signaling at once is pressure from all directions.
  • Use the same phrasing and the same sequence every turn: name, sit, command, That's it, Yes.
  • Between turns, settle the dog rather than leaving it in an activated state.
Expect a warm-upThe first one or two handlers in a rotation get the rough edges. The dog adapts as the rotation continues. That is normal, not a reflection of those handlers.

The Idle State

Ready but not working.

The idle state is the dog settled, comfortable, not pursuing its own interests, and still prepared to go. A dog toy across the room is available and the dog leaves it alone. This is a trained state, not a personality trait.

A young driven dog often cannot hold it. Every pause feels like the end of the world, so it barks, whines, or paces because it cannot stand being unoccupied for twenty seconds. With time the dog learns the work always comes back and the pause stops carrying weight.

Where to practice itBetween turns in a rotation. Ask for a settle, hold it, then resume. The pause is the rep.

The "That's It" Cue

The only phrase you are allowed to repeat.

Commands get said once. That's it is encouragement, not a command, so you can say it as many times as you want. Use it to fuel the dog toward a goal after the command has been given.

How to use it

  1. Give the command one time. Example: Get it
  2. Switch entirely to That's it from that point forward.
  3. Play hot and cold with your tone. Energy up when the dog moves toward the goal. Energy down when the dog drifts away.
  4. Mark with Yes the moment the goal is reached, then reward.
Tone does the workA flat, monotone "that's it" gives the dog nothing. It will feel strange to raise your pitch and energy in a quiet room. Do it anyway. The tone is the fuel.

First-Ask Urgency

Say it once. Expect a response.

Repeating a command teaches the dog that delay is acceptable. Catcher's analogy: a deadline at 12:30 that gets accepted at 12:35, then 1:00, then 1:30. Once there is no structure, the deadline is whatever you decide. A dog that knows you will keep asking learns it can wait until the twenty-fifth ask. For a service dog this compounds into real-world failure: a veteran asks for help and the dog decides it has time.

The rule

  • Command once. Then wait.
  • If the dog does not respond, do not repeat. Encourage with That's it, or reset and re-present.
  • Reward only the standard you actually want.
Why this mattersWithout first-ask urgency the dog learns to negotiate: who gives up first, you asking or the dog pursuing what it wants? That is a battle you do not want to start.

Duration, Distance, Distraction

The three levers that make any skill harder. Raise one at a time.

DurationHow long the dog holds the behavior or the item.
DistanceHow far the dog works from you. Raising distance automatically raises duration.
DistractionWhat else is competing for attention: people, dogs, smells, objects.

Progression rule

  1. Build the skill in a quiet, controlled space with all three levers at minimum.
  2. Raise one lever. Keep the other two low.
  3. If the dog fails, drop back one step and take smaller increments forward.
  4. When you move to a harder environment, start with the verbal command. If needed, fall back to the hand signal, then to the lure. Then work back up.

Commands

Go Say Hi · Polite Greeting

Cue: Go say hi · No treats involved

A service dog meets strangers constantly. Licking, leaning, and stretching the neck into someone are all things the dog has to check before doing. Polite greeting means the dog asks first and reads whether the person is inviting it.

Steps

  1. Handler releases the dog with Go say hi.
  2. The person greets, talks to, and pets the dog. No treats. This interaction is not a transaction.
  3. If the dog starts licking or leaning and the person does not want it, they lift their hands up and away. The dog deactivates and backs off.
  4. Rotate to the next person.
Watch forA big-bodied dog will lean its weight into people or stretch its neck across them. A dog eager to meet everyone will also rush from person to person. Both need slowing down.

Sit

Cue: Sit · Introduced Session 1

Steps

  1. Lure. Use a treat to guide the dog into the sit. The moment the rear settles, say Yes and reward.
  2. Hand only. Same motion, empty hand. Mark and reward from the pouch.
  3. Verbal. Say Sit once. Mark and reward.
StandardReward only a sit performed to the standard you want. Partial effort does not earn the treat.

Here

Cue: Here · The dog comes directly to the handler

Steps

  1. Say the dog's name. Wait for direct eye contact with you, not with the treat pouch.
  2. Mark the eye contact with Yes and reward.
  3. Say Here once and point to the space in front of you.
  4. Encourage the whole way in with That's it.
  5. Mark arrival with Yes and reward.

Touch

Cue: Touch · The dog pushes your hand with its nose

Steps

  1. Present your hand within easy reach of the dog's nose.
  2. Say Touch once.
  3. Wait for solid contact. A sniff or a near-miss does not count.
  4. Mark the real push with Yes and reward.
  5. If the dog misses, do not repeat the command. Re-present the hand and wait.
Why force mattersTouch is the base for every push task: handicap door buttons, closing refrigerator and cabinet doors. Build real pressure from the start.

Touch Button

Cue: Touch or Go touch · Uses an Easy Button-style clicker

The button teaches the dog to push hard enough to actually activate an object. It also makes the push measurable: either it clicks or it does not.

Stage 1 · Handler holds the button

  1. Hold the button in front of the dog.
  2. Say Touch once.
  3. When the button clicks, say Yes and reward.
  4. Pass the button to the next handler and repeat.

Stage 2 · Button at a distance

  1. Have someone hold the button across the room.
  2. Say the dog's name. Mark eye contact with Yes and reward. You want the dog connected to you before you send it.
  3. Point and say Go touch one time.
  4. From here use only That's it. Watch the dog's eyes. Energy up when it locks on the button, energy down when it drifts.
  5. When the button clicks, say Yes and reward.
Read the eyesThe dog's eyes tell you where it is going before its feet do. Your encouragement should track the eyes, not the body.

Cart Push · Mobility Assist

Cue: Go touch aimed at a cart · Introduced Session 3

Mobility dogs learn to give a wheelchair a nudge of momentum over a curb or an uneven surface. Not full pushing, just the extra push where it is needed. A shopping cart is a good teaching tool because it rolls and shifts, so the dog has to learn to push something unstable.

Setup

  • Mark a target zone on the cart with tape so the dog has a specific spot to aim at.
  • Standard is two significant pushes before the marker.

Steps

  1. Call the dog in. Here, then Sit.
  2. Point and say Go touch one time.
  3. After the first push, encourage with That's it to re-engage the dog for the second.
  4. Once you have real movement on the second push, say Yes and reward.
Handle chewingA frustrated dog will grab the cart handle. This is self-soothing, not disobedience. The jump from a button in your palm to an unstable rolling object is large, and the confusion floods in. Redirect with That's it and the dog will regroup and return to the task. Stay silent and the handle gets chewed, because the dog has no grounded support to come back to.
Task-definition riskA dog that is comfortable with the cart as a fun object will blend chewing and pushing. Keep the tasks isolated until the dog can hold two separate definitions for the same object.

Get It · Retrieval

Cue: Get it · Core mobility task · Introduced Session 2

Retrieval conserves the veteran's energy. Mobility limits mean every trip across a room costs from a limited daily tank. The dog fetching a phone, remote, or card saves that energy for what matters.

Why start with a bone-shaped toy

A dog already understands its mouth as a tool for eating, chewing, and carrying. A bone-shaped object is instantly recognizable and motivating, so you start with interest already on your side. Then you teach a different way of treating it: hold, do not chew, hand it back.

Stage 1 · One-second hold

  1. Ask for a Sit directly in front of you. Mark and reward.
  2. Hold the item by both knobby ends so the only available grip is the center.
  3. Angle the item slightly upward. Gravity carries it to the back of the mouth for a better hold. Presented flat or downward, the dog grabs with front teeth and the hold is weak.
  4. Say Get it once and release for about one second.
  5. Say Yes. The dog opens its mouth for the treat and the item drops back to you.
  6. Keep your hands under the dog's chin to catch the drop. Hands held out front give the dog nowhere to deliver.
Why one secondThe longer the dog holds the item, the more likely it decides to chew. Start with the shortest possible duration so the only interaction it learns is the one you want.

Stage 2 · Add duration

  1. Extend the hold to three seconds, then five.
  2. Mark and reward only clean holds with no chewing.

Stage 3 · Add distance

  1. Call the dog to you with Here. Mark eye contact.
  2. Place or drop the item across the room.
  3. Point and say Get it one time.
  4. Encourage the entire return trip with That's it, varying tone and energy.
  5. Hold a hand out to receive the item.
  6. Mark the handover with Yes and reward.
Presence helpsIf the dog is not committing to the return, stand up. A taller, more visible handler is easier to orient to across a room.
The drop problemIf the item hits the floor mid-return the difficulty spikes. On the ground it looks like a chew toy again. Ramp your encouragement immediately and use the dog's momentum to redirect it toward you.

Stage 4 · Add distraction

  1. Run the same retrieve with people, smells, or objects in the path.
  2. Do not lower the standard. Raise your encouragement instead.
  3. If the dog stalls at a specific spot, check for a real aversion before assuming distraction. See Fear and Avoidance.

Stage 5 · Generalize to new objects

  1. Once distance and duration are solid, swap in a new object: remote, phone, card.
  2. Run the same Get it sequence with no other changes.
  3. Expect fast transfer. The dog has learned the task, not the toy.
Handling awkward gripsA dog fueled by heavy encouragement will sometimes grab an item at a bad angle and then struggle to deliver it. That resolves with experience. Do not correct it.

Give and Drop It

Cues: Give and Drop it · The two ways a retrieve ends

Retrieval has three phrases. Get it sends the dog. Give hands the item to a person. Drop it releases the item into a place, such as a chair or a shopping cart.

Give

  1. Call the dog in, ask for a Sit.
  2. Point and say Get it once.
  3. Encourage the whole trip with That's it.
  4. Say Give with a hand out to receive.
  5. Mark the handover with Yes and reward.
The dog must complete the handoverDo not pick a dropped item up for the dog. Let it re-grip and hand it over. That is the whole point of the rep.

Drop It

  1. Send the dog toward the drop target with the item in its mouth.
  2. Point at the exact spot.
  3. Time the cue to the dog's nose. Say Drop it as the nose reaches the front edge of the target, so the head is over the spot when the item releases.
  4. If the dog holds on after the cue, say Yes immediately. "Yes" means food is coming, and the dog will let go to make room.
  5. Reward.
Timing is the hard partCue too early and the item lands short. Cue on an edge and it bounces off. A shopping cart is a small, finicky target and will produce real frustration. Make setup adjustments for the dog rather than raising the standard.
Paws on the targetIf the dog starts climbing the chair with its front paws, shorten the approach and mark before it reaches the target. Do not wait for it to sort itself out.

Labeled Retrieval

Cues: Get the phone, Get the remote, Get the card, Get my medicine

Once Get it is solid, each object gets its own label. The goal is five items sitting together on a coffee table and the dog bringing back the one you named, every time.

Steps

  1. Place a single item in one predictable spot. A chair at roughly the dog's head height keeps the approach angle simple.
  2. Call the dog in. Here, then Sit.
  3. Point and say the labeled command once. Get the phone.
  4. Encourage with That's it through the pickup and the return.
  5. Say Give. Mark the handover with Yes and reward.
  6. Run the item until it is clean, then swap to the next one.

Unpredictable positions

Items in real life are not staged on a chair at head height. A veteran who cannot bend after a knee replacement drops a phone on the floor. An item slides under a table. The dog has to solve a grip it has never used before, without hesitating.

  • Once a labeled item is clean from a predictable spot, move it. Floor, under furniture, odd angles.
  • Watch for the dog re-gripping in a completely new way. That flexibility is the actual goal, not the tidy rep.
  • Encourage through the fumbling. Do not reset the rep because the first grip failed.

Fading the Food

Treats are scaffolding, not the reason the dog works.

The dog does not need a treat for every rep forever. Once the relationship and the trust are established, the food gets drawn back gradually. The dog keeps performing because the partnership is the motivation.

SequencingBuild the behavior on food first. Fade it only after the bond and the reliability are there. Pulling the reward early undercuts both.

Tug · Planned Next Skill

Cue: Go tug · Not yet introduced

A knotted rope made to hang on a doorknob or handle. The dog pulls it to open a door or a refrigerator.

Why it mattersTug is the missing link in a chained task. The dog already knows Touch to close a door and Get it to retrieve. Add tug and it can open the fridge, take out a water bottle, close the fridge, and deliver it. The handler never touches the fridge, just runs a short sequence of commands.

PTSD Response · Planned Next Skill

Not yet introduced in sessions

The baseline behavior is nudging. The handler's leg starts shaking under stress or anxiety, and the dog nudges it to interrupt and ground them.

The personalization problem

  • Not everyone signals the same way. It may be a shaking leg, fixing the hair, rubbing an elbow, or holding the hands.
  • The dog has to learn the specific body-language shift that belongs to its handler.
  • A dog already determined to get your attention picks this up faster. A newer dog needs the behavior built from the start.
Why the intake forms matterParticipants sharing how the dog affects their own emotional moments is the data that shapes this. A dog that reads flat across the board tells you nothing about whether it taps into human emotion.

Handling and Behavior

Personality Types

Same curriculum. Opposite handling.

Dogs get sorted into personality categories, and the category decides which direction you push. Two dogs can run the identical exercise and need the exact opposite thing from you.

Activated vs. Inhibited

Two ways the same emotion comes out.

Every emotion a dog feels gets expressed one of two ways. This is separate from personality type, though the two often line up.

ActivatedOutward and expressive. Whining, huffing, barking, nudging, pacing, lunging. The dog throws everything on the table.
InhibitedClosed off and internalized. Quiet, avoidant, holding it close to the chest until it builds up and bursts.

The tradeoff

  • Activated is easy to read and taxing to manage. You know exactly how the dog feels, and it is a lot to hold.
  • Inhibited is easy to miss. Nothing surfaces until it overwhelms the dog internally, so the weight lands heavier and more unexpectedly.
  • Neither is better. Know which one you are working with and what it will hide from you.
Regulation is trainableAn activated dog can learn to whine or huff without escalating it into a demand. The goal is not silencing the expression, it is keeping it proportional.

Substitute Behaviors

What a dog does when the task gets too hard.

Raise the difficulty and confusion floods in. A driven dog wants that unwanted feeling gone immediately, so it grabs the nearest thing to offload it onto. Chewing a cart handle mid-task is not disobedience. It is regulation aimed at the wrong object.

The sequence to watch for

  1. The dog is focused and excited heading into the task.
  2. The task is harder than expected. The dog has to slow its own excitement.
  3. Body position goes wrong. Nose is not lined up, angle is off.
  4. Confusion piles onto frustration. The dog is lost.
  5. It grabs the nearest object and starts chewing.

What to do

  • Encourage with That's it. The dog hears that you still need it and regroups.
  • Stay silent and the chewing continues, because there is no grounded support to return to.
  • Long term, give the dog an appropriate outlet to offload onto, then send it back to the task.

Fear and Avoidance

Never force a dog through an unresolved fear.

How to spot it

  1. Watch for a dog that works fine in general but stalls on one specific task or one specific spot.
  2. Note whether the dog can route around the trigger. Avoidance that only appears when the task forces direct contact is the tell.
  3. Check for scent causes. Stress and anxiety pheromones from a prior dog's indoor accident can linger in carpet and read as aversive.

How to handle it

  1. Stop the task. Move the setup away from the trigger.
  2. Run the same task in a clean spot so the dog gets a success.
  3. Log the reaction in the dog's behavioral notes.
  4. Plan gradual desensitization for a future session. Not today.
The linePositive reinforcement means compliance under stress is not the goal. If heavy emotion is blocking the dog, the answer is patience and a different day, not pressure.
For handlersA failed rep is not a reflection of your relationship with the dog. Distance, smells, and other people are exterior challenges. Treat a setback as diagnostic information for the next attempt.

Patience and Demand Barking

The clock stops until the dog drops the idea.

Dogs learn fast that a voice gets attention. Demand barking works the first time you react to it, and then it works forever. Same with popping up from a stay.

The reset cycle

  1. Ask for the position. Down or Stay.
  2. The moment the dog breaks it, reset to the start. No reaction, no correction, no words.
  3. Repeat. The dog learns that forcing the issue only spins it back to where it started.
  4. When the dog settles, continue the session.

For barking

  • Do not look at the dog. Do not speak. Any reaction is the win the dog wanted.
  • If you need an alternative, hand over a chew or toy so the energy has somewhere to go.
Nothing negative happensThe reset is not punishment. The clock just stops. Nothing bad, nothing good, until the dog changes its mind.

Selfish vs. Unselfish Path

Let the dog choose. Then make your path the more attractive one.

At every fork the dog can serve itself or cooperate. A dog that repeatedly chooses itself in small moments will choose itself in big ones. The grocery store version: the veteran needs help reaching a shelf, and the dog goes for the meat counter instead.

The approach

  1. Do not block the selfish path. Let the dog stand at the fork.
  2. Give the command once.
  3. Pour encouragement into the cooperative path with That's it.
  4. Let the dog make the choice. Mark and reward heavily when it chooses cooperation.
  5. Do not get frustrated when it chooses itself. Note it and set up the next rep better.
GoalThe dog decides that helping you is worth more than the thing it wants. That decision has to be the dog's, not yours.

Equipment and Treats

Harness

  • Blue-9 harness
  • Front attachment ring. Discourages pulling by turning the dog rather than giving it forward leverage.

Training tools

  • Treat pouch, worn on the handler
  • Easy Button-style clicker for push tasks
  • Bone-shaped toy for early retrieval
  • Shopping cart with a taped target zone, for mobility push work
  • Knotted tug rope made to hang on a doorknob or handle

Retrieval objects

  • Old broken cell phone
  • TV remote
  • Hotel key card
  • Medicine bottle

Real items, chosen so each one presents a different shape, texture, and weight problem.

Treats

  • Cheerios
  • "Holly Bear" treats, roughly oyster-cracker sized

Both are low calorie and highly motivating, which matters across a full session with many reps.

The Team Training Phase

Three to four weeks, after the dog is matched to a handler.

Week 1 · BondThe umbilical process. Handler and dog attached at the hip.
Weeks 2–3 · CommandsAll 80 commands, plus expectations and troubleshooting.
Week 4 · UniquenessSkills personalized to the handler's actual life.

The umbilical process

Emotion travels both directions down the leash. Every stress, joy, and frustration the handler feels reaches the dog. By the end of week one the dog knows its handler better than anyone, because it has learned which slight change in body language leads where.

What week four covers

  • Retrieval items specific to that person. Some electric vehicles use a credit-card key instead of a fob, so that becomes a labeled item.
  • PTSD response tuned to that person's stress signals.
  • Anything else unique to how that handler lives.
Commands are taught to the whole householdWhen a dog is placed in a family environment, everyone learns the commands during team training, along with the expected result, the expected process, and how to troubleshoot when it does not go perfectly.

How Dogs See

Useful context for choosing training objects.

  • Primarily grayscale. A full spectrum from black through gray to white.
  • A limited range of blues and greens on top of that.
  • No red or orange.

This is why a tennis ball is so engaging. The dog can actually see its vibrance. Worth keeping in mind when picking targets and objects a dog needs to locate at a distance.

Non-Negotiable Rules

  1. Every "Yes" gets a reward. No exceptions.
  2. Commands are said once. Only That's it repeats.
  3. Reward only the standard. Partial effort does not earn the treat.
  4. Do not repeat a command when the dog gets it wrong. Wait. Re-present. Mark only the correct behavior.
  5. Never force a dog through fear. Back off and desensitize another day.
  6. Step backward when the dog struggles. Drop a layer or a difficulty lever.
  7. Setbacks are data, not failures. There is no exam and no grade.

Session 1 · Foundations

Positive Reinforcement Fundamentals

Dog: William · Veteran PTSD and mobility service dog

Three-layer method Yes marker Sit Here Touch Consistency

What was covered

  • The three-layer training structure: lure, hand motion only, verbal command.
  • The Yes marker and the rule that it is always followed by a reward.
  • Training across environments: start with no distractions, then step up gradually, falling back a layer when needed.
  • Commands introduced: Sit, Here, Touch.
  • Expanding Touch using an Easy Button-style clicker toward push-based tasks: handicap door buttons, refrigerator doors, cabinet doors.
  • Consistency rules: reward only to standard, do not repeat a command on a wrong response, wait, re-present, mark only the correct behavior.

Equipment

  • Blue-9 harness with front attachment ring.
  • Treats: Cheerios and "Holly Bear" treats.

Key takeaways

  • Build behaviors in progressive layers.
  • Never break the association between "Yes" and a reward.
  • Train without distraction first, then increase difficulty.
  • Step backward whenever the dog struggles.
  • Consistency produces reliable behavior.
